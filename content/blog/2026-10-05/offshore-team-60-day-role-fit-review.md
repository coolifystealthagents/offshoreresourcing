# Run a 60-day role-fit review before expanding an offshore team

Two months of work can reveal whether an offshore role has a coherent purpose, but only if the review looks beyond activity. A busy coordinator may be compensating for broken intake. A manager may call the role successful while spending hours correcting outputs privately. Expansion should follow evidence that the work, controls, and support model fit together.

The review is an operating decision, not a substitute for the employer's performance or employment processes. It examines whether to continue, repair, narrow, or expand the role lane.

## Reconstruct what the role actually did

Compare the approved brief with completed work. List outputs by type, volume, source, authority, reviewer, and outcome. Identify tasks added informally and planned tasks that never arrived. A role cannot be judged fairly against work it was not enabled or asked to perform.

Separate recurring delivery from one-time setup. The first 60 days may contain access delays, instruction building, training, and unusual manager attention. Record those conditions so the next-period estimate is not based on a distorted average.

## Review useful output and quality

For each major output, report eligible volume, completed volume, first-review acceptance, returns by reason, correction acceptance, and reopen rate. Sample evidence rather than relying on totals. Check whether sources, approvals, exceptions, and closure are traceable.

Look for learning across time. Did repeated errors decline after feedback? Did new error types appear as the work became more complex? Is quality stable across reviewers and shifts? Averages can hide an early ramp or a recent deterioration.

Distinguish employee-controlled defects from missing sources, unavailable access, conflicting instructions, and delayed review. Both matter, but they require different action.

## Measure waiting and manager effort

Break elapsed time into active handling, waiting for requester evidence, waiting for manager decision, waiting for system access, and correction. A queue that waits mostly onshore will not improve simply by adding another offshore specialist.

Estimate manager time spent assigning, answering, reviewing, correcting, and making reserved decisions. Compare it with the prior process and intended design. Some review is necessary; repeated reconstruction suggests the evidence pack or role boundary needs repair.

Ask whether reviewer coverage matches the role's schedule. If feedback routinely crosses several time zones without a defined window, redesign the handoff or add a qualified backup.

## Examine exceptions and authority

Count routine cases, correctly paused items, complete escalations, missed escalations, and unnecessary escalations. Review the highest-consequence cases individually. The role should keep standard work moving and surface genuine decisions without hiding risk.

Compare actual actions with the decision-rights charter. Remove unused permissions and correct any mismatch between technical access and business authority. Accurate work does not automatically justify payment, contract, personnel, policy, or security approval rights.

Identify recurring exceptions that can become documented rules, but require the authorized owner to approve the change. Past manager approvals do not silently create standing authority.

## Check workload shape and capacity

Plot arrivals and completions by week and work type. Include peaks, seasonality, rework, meetings, maintenance, leave, and downtime. Determine whether the role has a stable core or receives unrelated leftovers from multiple teams.

Look at queue age and work in progress, not only throughput. High completion with a growing old backlog signals prioritization or capacity trouble. Low utilization may mean insufficient demand, blocked inputs, excessive specialization, or an incorrect schedule.

Before adding headcount, identify the constrained stage. It may be sourcing, access, reviewer decisions, or customer evidence rather than production capacity.

## Ask the people closest to the handoff

Have the offshore employee, manager, reviewer, and key requester answer the same questions: What outcome is clear? Where does work wait? Which instruction conflicts? Which exceptions repeat? What should stop, continue, or change?

Request examples. General satisfaction is useful context but weak operating evidence. Protect personnel-sensitive feedback and do not expose private comments in a shared process report.

Compare perspectives. If the manager believes decisions are prompt while queue data shows long waits, inspect the clock definition and records. If requesters bypass intake, repair the path before blaming execution.

## Choose among five outcomes

Continue the current lane when demand, quality, controls, and support are stable. Repair enablement when access, sources, examples, or review are the main constraint. Narrow the role when tasks lack a coherent outcome or carry unsupported authority. Expand scope when adjacent work uses the same sources, skills, controls, and reviewers. Add capacity when sustained eligible demand exceeds demonstrated capacity after bottlenecks are addressed.

Write the evidence and assumptions behind the choice. Expansion should specify new outputs, volume, access, training, acceptance criteria, exceptions, reviewer capacity, and pilot period. Do not add a second person to an undocumented role and expect consistency.

## Set the next review

Translate the decision into owned actions with dates. Update the role brief, access map, instructions, examples, decision rights, and quality sample. Archive superseded versions. Tell affected requesters how intake or service expectations change.

Choose a new observation window appropriate to the change. A small instruction repair may need several comparable cases; a new high-risk lane needs supervised practice and closer review. Define stop conditions before launch.

Protect operational and personal data with approved storage, limited access, and retention. Use primary privacy and security guidance as input to qualified review rather than presenting a role-fit scorecard as legal advice.

The 60-day review is useful when it produces a concrete operating choice and exposes who must act next. It should prevent premature scaling while preserving a path to expand a role that genuinely works.
