# Turn a founder's informal process into an offshore-ready role brief

A founder often performs a process quickly because years of context have collapsed into instinct. They know which customer message matters, which supplier promise is credible, and which exception can wait. Writing “manage operations” in a role brief transfers none of that judgment. The first step is to observe the work closely enough to separate repeatable administration from founder-owned decisions.

The goal is not to document every thought. It is to give a Philippines-based specialist a coherent outcome, reliable inputs, bounded authority, and a reachable reviewer.

## Capture real events, not an idealized procedure

Observe several recent cases from trigger to closure. Record what arrived, where it came from, what the founder checked, actions taken, choices made, people consulted, output, and evidence. Include an ordinary case, an incomplete request, and an exception. Screen recordings can help, but convert them into a searchable operating record and handle any personal or confidential data under policy.

Ask “what made you choose that?” whenever the founder skips a step or changes direction. The answer may reveal a rule, a risk signal, or an undocumented commercial decision. Mark each one rather than pretending the path was linear.

## Separate facts, rules, preferences, and judgment

Facts come from authoritative records: dates, quantities, approved terms, or customer status. Rules define permitted actions and escalation triggers. Preferences affect presentation but not correctness. Judgment weighs competing outcomes and usually needs a named owner.

This classification prevents a common handoff failure. If the founder's preference is presented as a critical control, the new hire wastes time perfecting minor formatting. If a consequential judgment is presented as a routine step, the hire may make commitments without authority.

For every decision, state whether the specialist may execute, recommend, pause, or escalate. Add thresholds only when their units, exceptions, and owner are clear.

## Define one role outcome

Turn the process into a one-line result: “Maintain a complete supplier-request queue and prepare exceptions for founder decision,” or “Prepare qualified meeting options from approved requests and preserve commitments for executive approval.” Then list the recurring outputs that support it.

Avoid a miscellaneous list assembled from tasks the founder dislikes. A role needs enough related work to build fluency and a clear way to measure value. If two tasks use different systems, reviewers, data, and decisions, they may belong in separate lanes.

Estimate volume using a representative period. Include peaks, rework, waiting, meetings, and maintenance of the process itself. A count of completed items alone understates exception and coordination time.

## Build the source map

For each output, identify the source of truth, required fields, access owner, update frequency, and what to do when sources conflict. Eliminate private inboxes and local documents where possible. If the founder is the only source, define a structured intake rather than leaving the specialist to reconstruct requests from chat.

Design least-privilege access around the assigned actions. Use individual accounts and multifactor authentication. A specialist who prepares a decision may need read access without approval or payment rights. Test one permitted and one prohibited action before live work begins.

## Write exceptions before the happy path

List cases that make the founder slow down: missing evidence, unusual customer requests, changed bank details, conflicting deadlines, contractual language, sensitive personal data, or requests above a threshold. State the safe holding action, decision owner, evidence packet, response expectation, and backup.

Use examples with identifying details removed. A good example shows why an item was exceptional and how it was resolved. A bad example provides only the final answer, inviting the hire to copy it into a different context.

## Turn the record into a hiring brief

The brief should cover outcome, recurring tasks, excluded decisions, systems, working-hour needs, volume, acceptance criteria, exception path, manager responsibilities, and first-month milestones. Distinguish required skills from tools that can be taught. Ask candidates for evidence related to the actual work.

Create a fictional work sample from one bounded output. Supply realistic inputs and an exception. Score source use, accuracy, communication, and judgment against a rubric written in advance. Do not use candidate work as free production output.

## Test the role with a pilot

Before expanding scope, run a controlled set of cases. The founder or process owner should review quickly and record reasons for returns. Measure first-review acceptance, correct escalations, waiting by owner, missing-source frequency, and founder time spent on decisions versus reconstruction.

If the founder remains the bottleneck, determine why. The role may need clearer rules, a backup decision owner, or better intake. Adding more offshore hours will not solve a queue that waits for undocumented founder judgment.

At the end of the pilot, keep, revise, or remove tasks individually. Update access to match the approved lane. Preserve founder-owned decisions explicitly instead of treating independent execution as the only sign of success.

## Maintain the brief as the company changes

Assign an owner and version. Review it when products, customers, systems, policies, or authority change. Archive superseded instructions so the specialist does not choose between conflicting copies. Sample actual records to confirm practice still matches the brief.

Protect personal and confidential data during capture and training. Link to approved systems instead of creating broad exports. The Philippine National Privacy Commission and NIST provide useful primary materials for qualified privacy and security reviews.

The brief is ready when a candidate can understand the outcome, a manager can identify reserved decisions, and a reviewer can assess a completed item from evidence. It should make the founder's contribution more focused, not recreate the founder in checklist form.
