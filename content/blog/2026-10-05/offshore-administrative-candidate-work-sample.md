# Create a work-sample exercise for offshore administrative candidates

An administrative work sample should answer a narrow hiring question: can this candidate produce the kind of bounded output the role requires from the information the role will actually receive? It should not reward secret knowledge, free production work, or familiarity with one company's unwritten habits.

For a Philippines offshore role, the exercise can test remote handoffs and written clarification as well as administrative accuracy. The fairest design uses fictional data, the same instructions for every candidate, a reasonable time box, and a scoring guide written before submissions arrive.

## Choose one outcome from the role brief

Start with a frequent, important output: a conflict-checked schedule proposal, a cleaned request queue, a candidate interview pack, or a status summary. Avoid combining every duty into a miniature workday. A focused sample produces evidence that interviewers can compare.

Map the source material, permitted actions, expected output, common exceptions, and reviewer. If the job requires the person to prepare decisions but not make them, preserve that boundary. The exercise might ask a candidate to flag a conflict and recommend options, not decide which executive commitment to cancel.

Remove real names, customer records, credentials, and confidential documents. Invent a coherent scenario with enough detail to complete the task. Tell candidates that the data is fictional and should be used only for the assessment.

## Make the instructions complete but not overdirected

Provide the business goal, available records, time box, output format, submission method, and assumptions the candidate may make. State whether questions are allowed and how answers will be shared. If asking a good question matters in the role, include a genuine ambiguity instead of withholding essential information at random.

Distinguish an exception from a trick. For example, a calendar request may conflict with a protected focus block and lack a priority label. A good candidate can flag the conflict, identify the missing decision, and prepare two options. An impossible date hidden in tiny text tests proofreading, but it says little about operational judgment unless such checks are actually part of the job.

Set a time expectation that reflects the task and candidate burden. Explain whether completion or prioritization matters more. If the sample is substantial, determine an appropriate compensation and consent approach through qualified hiring and legal owners.

## Build a fictional scheduling exercise

Give the candidate a short calendar, five meeting requests, working-hour rules, travel buffers, a priority guide, and an approved email style. Include one complete request, one missing attendee time zone, one double booking, one request outside permitted hours, and one item that requires manager choice.

Ask for a proposed schedule, an exception list, two drafted messages, and a brief handoff to the manager. The candidate should not invent the absent time zone or silently move a protected commitment. They should show what can be scheduled, what must wait, and what exact decision is needed.

This exercise reveals source discipline, prioritization, clarity, and respect for authority. It also gives the interviewer concrete material for follow-up: Why did you pause that request? What would you verify first? How would you update the record after the manager decides?

## Score observable evidence

Create dimensions before reviewing: source accuracy, completeness, prioritization, exception recognition, communication, traceability, and adherence to authority. Define what strong, acceptable, and insufficient evidence looks like for each. Weight the dimensions according to the role rather than giving presentation polish the largest share by default.

For source accuracy, a strong submission preserves dates, names, and constraints from the packet. For exception judgment, it identifies conflicts and routes them without inventing approval. For communication, it drafts concise messages that state the needed action and avoid unsupported promises. For traceability, another person can see how the proposal came from the inputs.

Do not score accent, idiom, or decorative formatting unless a clearly justified job requirement makes it relevant. Evaluate understandable communication against the work, using the same standard for every candidate.

## Calibrate reviewers before live use

Have reviewers independently score two sample responses, then compare reasons. Resolve differences in the rubric. One reviewer may treat a missing field as minor while another sees it as disqualifying; candidates should not bear the cost of that hidden disagreement.

Require comments tied to evidence. “Not strategic” is not useful. “Scheduled the meeting despite the stated approval requirement and did not flag the conflict” is reviewable. Keep interview discussion separate from the initial scoring so a persuasive colleague does not erase the written evidence.

Periodically check outcomes by reviewer and candidate group with qualified people owners. A consistent score does not prove a fair or valid assessment. Investigate whether unnecessary requirements create barriers and whether the sample predicts performance in the actual role.

## Protect candidate data and assessment integrity

Tell candidates what data is collected, why, who reviews it, how long it is retained, and how to use the appropriate privacy contact or process. Store submissions in an approved recruiting system with limited access. Do not reuse a candidate's work as company output.

Use equivalent versions if assessment content is circulating, but keep difficulty and scoring comparable. Record the version delivered. Accessibility accommodations should preserve the skill being assessed while changing barriers that are not part of the role; route requests through the appropriate hiring process.

The Philippine National Privacy Commission provides first-party Data Privacy Act resources, and the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission publishes employment-test guidance relevant to employers within its scope. Qualified owners should determine which requirements apply to the specific hiring relationship and locations.

## Debrief from the evidence

Use the interview to explore the submission, not to rescue it invisibly. Ask the candidate to explain assumptions, identify what they would check with more time, and respond to one changed fact. Record whether the new response demonstrates the target skill.

Combine the work sample with structured interview evidence and role requirements. Do not turn one exercise into a universal verdict. After hiring, compare assessment signals with actual early work in aggregate so the company can improve or retire weak measures.

A useful sample is small enough to respect the candidate, realistic enough to represent the role, and structured enough to support a defensible comparison. It should leave both sides with a clearer view of the work.
