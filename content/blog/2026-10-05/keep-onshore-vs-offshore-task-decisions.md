# Decide which tasks to keep onshore when building an offshore role

The wrong question is “What jobs can be offshored?” A job title contains work with different sources, consequences, authority, and review needs. An operations coordinator might safely maintain a queue and prepare exception summaries while contract interpretation remains with an onshore owner. A customer support specialist might resolve documented requests while refunds above a threshold stay with an authorized manager.

The practical unit of design is therefore the task and decision, not the title. Breaking a role into those units helps a buyer build a useful Philippines-based position without transferring authority accidentally or keeping so many approvals onshore that the new hire can do little but wait.

## Map the work before assigning a location

Observe a normal week and list recurring triggers, inputs, actions, outputs, decisions, exceptions, and approvals. Include work that currently lives in inboxes or memory. Record who supplies each input and who uses the output. A task that appears self-contained may depend on a manager answering five undocumented questions.

Write tasks as verb plus object: reconcile the daily order report, draft a response from the approved knowledge base, schedule an agreed meeting, prepare a candidate comparison, or update a compliance-document tracker. Labels such as “help with finance” conceal boundaries and make access design difficult.

Then separate production from decision rights. Preparing a comparison is not selecting a vendor. Recording an approved adjustment is not approving it. Drafting a customer response is not promising a remedy outside policy. These distinctions often create a coherent offshore lane while retaining accountable decisions with the business owner.

## Use six tests for every task

First, test repeatability. Does the task have a stable trigger, known inputs, and observable finish? A variable task can still be suitable if it has clear decision rules and an escalation route. “Use judgment” is not a process description.

Second, test authority. Can the person perform the task without binding the company, moving money, changing a sensitive master record, interpreting law, or making a reserved personnel decision? If not, split preparation from authorization or keep the decision with its qualified owner.

Third, test information exposure. Identify the minimum data needed, its approved system, export restrictions, and retention rules. Do not give a broad shared drive because it is easier than configuring role-based access. Use individual accounts, multifactor authentication, least privilege, logging where appropriate, and a removal owner.

Fourth, test consequence. What happens if the task is late, wrong, duplicated, or performed without approval? A recoverable formatting error differs from an incorrect bank-account change. Higher consequence may require pre-action review, tighter access, or an onshore owner even when the mechanics are simple.

Fifth, test handoff cost. Count the clarification, review, and rework needed to move the task between people and time zones. A five-minute action that needs three live conversations is not ready merely because instructions exist. Repair the source record or decision rule before transferring it.

Sixth, test reviewer capacity. An offshore employee cannot become independent if the accountable reviewer is unavailable. Estimate review volume, response expectations, backup coverage, and the evidence a reviewer needs. The bottleneck may be the onshore decision, not offshore execution.

## Place tasks into four operating lanes

The first lane is independent execution within defined authority. These tasks have approved inputs, clear acceptance criteria, controlled access, and a route for exceptions. Examples could include updating a tracker from an authoritative record or sending an approved status message.

The second lane is prepare and recommend. The offshore team member gathers evidence, applies a documented method, and presents options, but a named owner decides. Candidate comparisons, invoice discrepancies, and unusual support remedies often fit here.

The third lane is supervised practice. The task may become routine, but the employee or process has not yet demonstrated reliability. Every action receives review for a bounded sample, with explicit criteria for changing the control.

The fourth lane is retained decision or specialist work. This includes actions requiring licensed expertise, fiduciary authority, sensitive employee judgment, executive commitment, or risk acceptance. Offshore staff may support the record without representing themselves as the decision maker.

Avoid treating these lanes as permanent labels. A source-system change can make a previously routine task ambiguous. Strong evidence and stable controls may let supervised work become independent. Reassess when volume, policy, systems, authority, or consequence changes.

## Work through a realistic example

Imagine a founder wants an offshore assistant to “handle supplier invoices.” The task map reveals seven activities: receive invoices, check required fields, match them to purchase and receipt records, request missing evidence, summarize discrepancies, approve payment, and change supplier bank details.

Receiving, checking, matching, and requesting standard evidence may fit independent execution after training. Summarizing discrepancies fits prepare and recommend. Payment approval stays with the named finance owner. Bank-detail changes follow a separate fraud-control and independent-verification process. The resulting role is useful because it moves evidence work forward without hiding the reserved decisions.

Now check the handoff. If purchase orders are incomplete or receipts arrive through private messages, the offshore coordinator will spend the day chasing context. Fix those upstream records. Offshoring does not remove process disorder; it makes the disorder easier to see.

## Design time-zone use deliberately

Location can improve flow when the handoff is real. A Philippines-based employee can prepare a queue before an overseas reviewer begins work, or cover agreed customer hours. But “follow the sun” fails when every item waits for a synchronous answer.

Define an overlap window for coaching and exceptions. Outside it, specify safe work the employee can continue, the channel for urgent matters, and the next response time. Record schedules in both local zones and account for daylight-saving changes in the manager's country. Respect applicable employment terms and working-hour policies rather than treating location as permanent availability.

## Pilot a complete slice

Choose one outcome with enough volume to expose normal variation. Supply current instructions, representative examples, individual access, an exception taxonomy, and a named reviewer. Test ordinary items and awkward ones. Measure accepted outputs, reasons for return, correct escalations, waiting time by owner, and rework caused by missing inputs.

Do not judge the pilot only by hourly cost or total items. A cheap lane that creates hidden manager rework is not efficient. Compare the before-and-after path: elapsed time, touch count, decision delay, error recovery, and manager attention. Include the cost of maintaining instructions and reviews.

At the end, decide task by task. Continue some, revise others, and retain decisions that lack safe boundaries. Update access to match the final lane. The result should be a role with a meaningful outcome, not a bundle of miscellaneous chores rejected by everyone else.

## Questions for the final role brief

Can a new hire identify the source of truth? What may they do without asking? What must they never do without approval? Which exceptions stop the process? Who makes each decision, and who is backup? What evidence proves completion? How quickly will review arrive? Which permissions are required, and who removes them? What change triggers a new risk review?

Answers to those questions create a stronger hiring brief and a more honest workload estimate. They also make candidate evaluation more relevant because a work sample can test the actual lane rather than generic software familiarity.
