## Research question and decision supported

The question is how often offshore back office teams keep records past the disposal date set by policy or client instruction. The decision supported is whether the retention rule is usable, whether disposal is scheduled, and whether working copies escape the official system. A record can be retained for a legitimate legal hold, so the audit must separate an expired record from one that is protected. The method measures the record's status, not the team's intent.

State which record types and systems the audit covers. A customer ticket, an invoice, and a candidate file may have different retention periods and different owners. Report the scope beside the rate so the result is not treated as a complete privacy audit. The decision should follow the record type with the highest risk.

## Define the disposal date

The disposal date is the point at which the record should be removed, anonymized, or reviewed under the applicable policy. It may be a fixed period after the task closes, a contract end date, or a client instruction. Record the policy source and the owner who set it. If the policy does not give a precise date, record the rule used to calculate it. A date invented by the offshore team is not a valid basis for deletion.

Write examples of records with a fixed period, a contract-based period, and a legal hold. Include a record that must be kept longer because of a customer request. Review the examples with the privacy or legal owner before the audit. A shared definition prevents the reviewer from applying a personal preference about how long records should live.

## Draw the sample

Select records from a defined period and include every system in scope. Include the official system, the shared drive, and approved exports, because a record may be deleted from one place and remain in another. Exclude records that are still active or that fall under a documented hold. Record the record type, system, owner, and disposal date. If the volume is low, review every record and report the count.

Stratify by record type and system so one large archive does not dominate the result. Check that the sample includes records created by each role that handles the data. A sample drawn only from the current system may miss older copies on a shared drive. Keep the selection rule and the system list with the report.

## Measure retention past the date

Count records that remain accessible after the disposal date without a documented hold. Report the share by record type, system, and owner. Separate records that were never reviewed from records that were reviewed and deliberately kept. A record with no owner is a different problem from one with a documented exception. Show the age beyond the disposal date so a small number of very old records is visible.

Check whether the record is still used by an active process. A record past its date may be part of a live dispute or a recurring task. Record the reason and the owner before classifying it as a failure. Do not delete anything during the audit, because the review should not change the evidence it is measuring.

## Separate absence from inaccessibility

A record may be deleted from the official system but remain in a backup, an export, or a local folder. Test the locations named in the retention rule, not only the main application. If a location cannot be accessed, record it as inaccessible rather than compliant or noncompliant. If a system has no deletion function, note the limitation and the compensating control. The distinction changes the fix: an access problem needs permission or retention work, while an unmanaged copy needs a storage change.

Record the exact location and the error when access fails. Do not ask an administrator to remove a record during the audit. If the record is in a system the team does not own, note the system owner and the retention instruction. The reviewer's ability to find the record is part of the control.

## Compare by record type and system

Group the results by record type, system, and owner. Look for a system that has no disposal process or a record type whose policy is unclear. Compare the official system with shared drives and exports. A low rate in the official system with a high rate in a shared drive suggests that the control covers the wrong place. Read a few examples in each group before deciding what the pattern means.

Check whether the record type is covered by a client-specific rule that differs from the internal policy. A client may require a longer period, and the team may be following that instruction correctly. Report the rule source rather than assuming that every old record is a violation. Read the client instruction before changing the disposal date. Note whether the record is still linked from an active case, because that link may explain why the team kept it. The reviewer should record the reason rather than deleting the file during the audit.

## Test one disposal control

Choose the most common gap and change one control. If the shared drive has no review, add a scheduled disposal check with a named owner. If a system has no deletion function, document the limitation and the compensating step. If the policy is unclear, ask the owner to set the period before the team deletes anything. Test the control on a small set of records and confirm that it removes the record from every in-scope location.

Confirm that the change was delivered and understood. Check the schedule, the owner, and the instruction. A disposal calendar that nobody reviews is not a control. Compare the next sample by record type and system so the result is not driven by a one-time cleanup.

## Use the result to fix the retention rule

Record whether the rule should continue, be clarified, or be replaced. Name the owner of each open issue and the date it will be reviewed. Update the record inventory if a new system or export path was found. Keep the audit evidence with the rule so the next reviewer can see what was tested. A retention rule that is never audited becomes a statement of intent rather than an operating control.

Share the result with the team in plain language. Focus on where records live and what to do at the disposal date, not on blame for old files. Ask whether the team has a legitimate reason for keeping a record that the rule does not cover. The answer may reveal a policy gap that the owner needs to resolve.

## Limitations

The method depends on a record inventory and a disposal rule that are complete enough to test. A record may be retained for a legitimate reason that is not visible in the system, and a backup may keep a copy beyond the team's control. Reviewers may disagree about whether a record is accessible or held. Small samples and multiple systems make the rate unstable, and a one-time cleanup can improve the result without changing the underlying control.

A retained record is not automatically a privacy breach. It may be protected by a legal hold or a client requirement. Use the result to improve the retention rule and the disposal process, not to judge the team from a single file. Report the scope, the definitions, and any system change so the next audit is comparable.
