## Why evidence matters

Interviews alone make it difficult to compare candidates for distributed roles. An evidence matrix links each requirement to an observable signal, a consistent question, and a documented decision. It also helps a buyer distinguish language fluency, tool familiarity, judgment, and reliability instead of treating them as one vague impression.

## Matrix structure

| Requirement | Evidence | Consistency check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Written clarity | Short role-relevant sample | Same rubric for every candidate |
| Prioritization | Scenario response | Score reasoning, not confidence |
| Data care | Access and privacy scenario | Verify escalation judgment |
| Reliability | Reference or work history | Record what was verified |

ISO 30405 and the U.S. EEOC both support structured, job-related selection practices. The goal is not to remove human judgment. It is to make the judgment reviewable and connected to the work.

## Offshore context

Use examples that resemble the actual service: updating a customer record, organizing a research brief, or clarifying an incomplete request. Keep the exercise bounded and avoid asking for unpaid production work. A good resourcing partner should be able to show how candidates were assessed against the agreed role scope.

## Conclusion

The matrix is strongest when it exposes uncertainty. Mark evidence as verified, incomplete, or contradictory, and route unresolved questions to the named buyer. That creates a safer basis for selection than a single overall score.
