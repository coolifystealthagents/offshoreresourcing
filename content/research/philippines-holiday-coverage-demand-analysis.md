## What the analysis answers

Holiday coverage debates often start from a binary question: should the team work or not? A more useful question is which work must continue during a specific holiday and what coverage that work requires.

This note describes a short analysis that uses the queue, the customer commitments, and the contract to answer that question without guessing.

## Build the work list

List the recurring tasks and mark each as customer-facing, internal, or scheduled. Add the consequence of a one-day delay and the contractual or regulatory constraint that applies.

A task with no consequence and no constraint is a candidate for a holiday pause. A task with a customer commitment may need coverage even if the volume is low.

## Use historical demand

Pull the last two years of queue volume for the same holiday period. Separate requests that arrived because the office was closed from requests that would have arrived anyway.

Record the volume, the channel, and the outcome. A single prior year is context, not a forecast.

## Decide the coverage model

For each task, choose closed, skeleton, or normal coverage. Record the reason, the approver, and the compensation treatment under the employment arrangement.

Keep the decision consistent with the contract. A promise of closure that the contract does not support creates a different risk.

## Communicate the expectation

Publish the coverage calendar with the response time that applies. Customer-facing teams need the date and the exception path, not just an internal note.

## Limitations

Historical demand may not predict a new product launch or a regulatory deadline. The method estimates the routine case and requires a separate check for known events.
