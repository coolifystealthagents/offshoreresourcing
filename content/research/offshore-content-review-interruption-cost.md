## Research question

When an editor switches from claim verification to messages, urgent drafts, or administrative requests, how much time and quality are lost before focused review resumes? The outcome should include recovery and defect signals, not merely the number of interruptions.

## Evidence scope and method

Laboratory and workplace literature indicates that task switching can impose cognitive costs, but long-form editorial review differs from controlled experiments. For a limited period, reviewers can record the review stage, interruption class, duration, resumption time, and whether the article later needed a preventable correction. Compare interrupted and uninterrupted sessions within similar article classes. Do not install surveillance software or infer attention from online status.

## What to measure

Use median active review time, elapsed review time, time to resume, first-pass acceptance, and correction incidence. Record article length, evidence load, reviewer experience, and deadline pressure. A useful operational finding would show a repeatable interruption pattern that managers can route or batch differently.

## Limitations and safeguards

Self-reporting misses brief switches and can change behavior. Difficult articles may attract interruptions because they require questions, reversing the apparent direction of cause. Small defect counts make percentage changes unstable. Report ranges and raw counts, protect personnel privacy, and use findings to redesign intake rather than rank individuals.

## Conclusion

A two-week diary can reveal where review is repeatedly fragmented. Treat it as a queue-design study. If a protected review block improves acceptance without creating unsafe response delays elsewhere, test the practice for another cycle before making it routine.
