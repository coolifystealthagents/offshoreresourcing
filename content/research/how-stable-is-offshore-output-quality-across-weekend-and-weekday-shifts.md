## Research question and decision supported

The question is whether the quality of offshore output differs between weekend and weekday shifts after accounting for task type, volume, and review coverage. The decision supported is whether the team needs a different review standard, a different staffing mix, or a narrower scope on weekends. A raw comparison can mislead because weekend queues often contain different work and fewer reviewers. The method matches the samples before comparing the rates. It also separates a quality difference from a difference in how quickly the work is reviewed.

State whether the decision is about staffing, scope, review coverage, or training. A single comparison cannot answer all four. Choose the decision first and select the measure that informs it. Report the other outcomes as context rather than as additional conclusions.

## Define the comparison

Choose a quality measure that the team already records, such as acceptance at first review, rework rate, or severity-weighted findings. Define the weekend window and the weekday window using the same time zone and the same business calendar. State whether a Friday night item reviewed on Saturday belongs to the weekend or the weekday shift. Use the review date for the quality measure and the completion date for the work measure, and keep the two separate. A clear definition prevents a scheduling boundary from becoming an unexplained quality difference.

Write the shift boundary and the review-date rule in the method. Test the rule on items that cross midnight and items completed on Friday but reviewed on Monday. If the boundary is ambiguous, the comparison will be too. Keep the same rule for every period in the study.

## Build a matched sample

Select items from both periods and match them by task type, complexity band, customer segment, and whether the item was routine or exceptional. If exact matching is not possible, use stratification and report the distribution. Include the same number of items per stratum where the volume allows. Exclude items that were escalated before completion, because they follow a different review path. Record the worker, the reviewer, and the tenure of both. A sample that compares complex weekend work with routine weekday work will attribute the task difference to the shift.

Show the distribution of the matching variables for both periods. If the distributions differ, explain how the analysis accounts for the difference. A matched sample with a hidden imbalance can produce a misleading result. Report the strata that could not be matched.

## Control for staffing and review coverage

Weekend shifts often have fewer reviewers, a different supervisor, and a thinner escalation path. Record the number of reviewers on duty, the ratio of reviewers to producers, and the availability of the named approver. Check whether the same reviewer sees the item at first pass and at final acceptance. A quality difference may come from review capacity rather than from the worker's output. Include the time from completion to first review and the number of items waiting at the end of the shift. These variables help explain a difference that a simple rate would hide.

Record the actual reviewer coverage, including absences and backups. A planned ratio is not evidence of the coverage that occurred. Note any shift where a supervisor was unavailable or a queue was reassigned. These events help explain a difference that the production data alone cannot show.

## Measure quality and review latency

Report the acceptance rate, the rework rate, and the severity mix for each period. Add the median and tail of the review latency and the number of items that missed a due point. Compare the same measures within each matched stratum, not only in the aggregate. Use a count alongside the percentage when a stratum is small. Show the number of items that were reviewed by a backup or a different reviewer. If weekend quality is lower but review latency is also longer, the review process may be the place to intervene rather than the production standard.

Keep the quality measure and the latency measure in separate columns. A lower acceptance rate with a longer review time may point to the review process rather than the work. Add the number of items that were reviewed more than once. This shows whether the difference is in production or in review.

## Account for workload and task mix

A weekend queue may be smaller but more volatile, with a higher share of urgent or unusual items. Compare the arrival pattern, the proportion of exceptions, and the amount of client input available. Check whether the weekday team has more time to ask questions or wait for a source record. If the weekend team handles the same task with less support, a quality gap may be an expected consequence of the design. Adjust the comparison for the mix before concluding that the shift is less capable. Do not use a single weekend as evidence of a stable pattern.

Compare the arrival pattern and the exception share for each period. A weekend with a system outage is not comparable to a normal weekend. Mark unusual periods and run the comparison with and without them. If the result changes, report both versions rather than choosing the more convenient one.

## Test whether the difference persists

Repeat the comparison over several weekends and weekdays so a holiday, a system outage, or one difficult account does not drive the result. Plot the rate by week and look for a consistent gap rather than one spike. If the gap appears only when a particular reviewer is absent, the issue is coverage rather than the shift. If it appears across task types and reviewers, the training or the instruction may need a weekend-specific review. Read a sample of findings from both periods and check whether the same standard was applied. Persistence is more useful than a single significant difference.

Use a simple run chart rather than a single test of significance. Look for a consistent direction across several weeks. If the gap appears only in one stratum, the aggregate result may be misleading. Report the pattern at the level where the decision will be made.

## Use the result to change one support

Choose a change that matches the explanation. If review coverage is thin, add a named backup or a shorter weekend sample. If the task mix is the cause, narrow the weekend scope or route unusual items to a weekday owner. If the instruction is unclear, add a worked example and a short calibration before the next weekend. Set a review date and repeat the matched comparison with the same definitions. Do not change the quality target and the staffing model at the same time unless the team can measure both. The goal is a reliable service every day, not an identical rate for its own sake.

Write the change as a support adjustment, such as a named backup reviewer or a narrower weekend scope. Set a review date and compare the same measures. Ask the team whether the change addressed the actual constraint. A support change that ignores the explanation will not improve the result.

## Limitations

The method depends on consistent quality records and a clear shift boundary. Weekend and weekday work may differ in ways that matching cannot capture, and the reviewer group may change between periods. Small samples produce unstable rates, and a holiday or outage can distort a single comparison. The analysis does not prove that shift timing causes a quality difference. Report the matching variables, the sample size, and the review coverage. Use the result to generate a testable explanation, not to label a shift or a group.

Shift timing is not randomly assigned, so the comparison cannot prove causation. Quality records may be inconsistent, and the reviewer group may change. A small sample or a holiday period can distort the result. Report the matching, the confounders, and the sample size. Use the finding to test an explanation, not to label a shift.
