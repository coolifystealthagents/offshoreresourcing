Schedule overlap is one of the most common sources of mismatch between an offshore role and the team it supports. A role can be advertised as full time and still fail because the person is never available when a decision is needed. The opposite is also possible: a team pays for a long overlap that is used mainly for status meetings. This protocol defines a way to evaluate the requirement from the work itself. Compare [offshore staffing services](/services) with the [research library](/research) when you translate this method into a review plan.

The method is intended for a manager who is scoping a new role or reviewing an existing one. It uses a calendar and a task inventory rather than a general preference for shared hours. The output is a documented overlap requirement with a cost range and a review date. It is not a formula that produces one correct schedule for every team.

## Research question and decision supported

The question is which tasks require live presence and how much overlap those tasks actually need. The decision supported is the shift pattern, the required overlap window, and the backup arrangement for work outside that window. A second decision is whether a task should be redesigned as an asynchronous handoff instead of requiring a meeting. Record the consequence of moving each event, because that consequence is what makes the requirement defensible.

The evaluation should separate the minimum overlap from the preferred overlap. The minimum is the time needed to keep commitments and decisions moving. The preferred is the extra time that improves collaboration or training. Mixing the two makes the requirement sound larger than it is and makes the cost harder to defend.

## Population and unit of analysis

Define the population as the recurring and scheduled tasks that involve both the offshore role and the onshore team. Include meetings, approvals, handoffs, and client calls. Exclude one-off events unless they recur often enough to shape the schedule. Include the preparation and follow-up time that the task creates, because those hours are part of the real load.

The unit is one scheduled event or one recurring task, not one person. A role may have several events that need the same overlap window. Group them by time and decision type so the schedule can be tested against the real demand. Do not average across unrelated tasks, because a short daily call and a long weekly review have different scheduling effects.

## Definitions and measures

Define overlap as the time when both the offshore role and the onshore decision owner are available for live communication. Define required overlap as the time needed for tasks that cannot be completed asynchronously within the commitment. Define preferred overlap as the time that improves coordination but is not essential. Record the time zone and the daylight saving rule for each side.

Measure the number of required events, their duration, their frequency, and their time window. Measure the preparation time and the follow-up time separately. Report the total required overlap in hours per week, the longest continuous block, and the number of days with an early or late start. These measures show the shape of the demand, not just the volume.

## Evidence and data dictionary

The data dictionary should include the event identifier, the task, the participants, the decision owner, the frequency, the duration, the time zone, the preparation time, and the follow-up time. Add a field for whether the event can be asynchronous and a field for the consequence of moving it. Add the current shift pattern so the gap between demand and coverage is visible. Add a field for the person who confirmed the event, because calendar entries are often stale.

Capture the evidence from the calendar and the task inventory, not from memory. If a meeting is described as essential, ask what decision it supports and what would happen if it moved. Preserve the answer. A meeting that has no decision and no consequence is a candidate for an asynchronous update.

## Sampling and collection

Use a four-week calendar window that includes a normal week, a peak week, and any holiday that affects the team. Collect the events from the calendar export and the task list. If the team has no reliable calendar, ask each participant to keep a simple log for two weeks. Record the collection method and the limitations.

Include the informal calls and chat escalations that do not appear on the calendar, because they often drive the real overlap need. Ask the team to note the time and reason for urgent live contact. A schedule that covers only the formal meetings may still fail when an exception arrives. The informal log should be small and time-bound.

## Reviewer independence and agreement

Have the manager and the offshore lead review the same demand list independently. Compare their judgments about which events are required and which can be asynchronous. A disagreement may reveal that the manager values a meeting for coordination while the lead sees it as a status update. Record the reason for each disagreement and resolve it with a decision.

Agreement on the list does not prove that the schedule is right. The team may agree that an event is required when it could be redesigned. Test one event in an asynchronous format and compare the outcome. The review should also ask whether the same decision could be made with a written pre-read and a shorter call.

## What the evidence can support

The evidence can support a shift pattern, a cutoff, a meeting redesign, or a decision to add a backup. It can show whether the overlap requirement is concentrated in one part of the day or spread across the week. It can also show whether the current schedule leaves enough focus time for the queue. These are concrete operating decisions.

The evidence cannot prove that a particular candidate will accept the schedule or that the arrangement will be sustainable over a year. It also cannot capture every informal coordination need. Report the assumptions and plan a review after the first month. A schedule that works on paper still needs a real test.

## Limitations and error

Calendar data is often incomplete. Meetings may be missing, durations may be wrong, and preparation time may be invisible. The informal log helps but adds recall error. Report the data source and the gaps. Do not present a precise overlap number when the underlying calendar is unreliable.

Another limitation is that the team may change its behavior during the review. People may attend meetings they normally skip or add calls to justify the requirement. Keep the review light and compare the result with the actual schedule after a month. A schedule decision based on a distorted calendar will not survive contact with the work.

## Sensitivity and alternatives

Rerun the calculation with the preferred overlap removed to see the minimum requirement. Rerun with the longest meeting converted to an asynchronous update. If the required overlap drops materially, the original schedule may be based on habit rather than need. Rerun with a different shift start to see how the overlap changes across daylight saving.

Consider alternatives before adding hours. A task may be moved to a different owner, split into a prepared step and a decision step, or handled with a written standard. A longer overlap is not the only answer. Name the alternative and the trade-off, including the cost and the risk of delay.

## Privacy, access, and retention

Use role labels and event identifiers in the working file. Avoid copying personal calendar details that are not needed for the schedule analysis. If the review includes client calls, use the client code rather than the client name. Keep the working file in a restricted location.

Set a retention period and remove the raw calendar export when the review is closed. Keep the final requirement and the method notes for the period required by the client agreement. Log access to the file. A schedule review should not become a surveillance record of individual working hours.

## Reporting template

Report the window, the events, the required and preferred overlap, the longest block, and the data limitations. Show the cost range for the shift options and the effect on focus time. Include the events that can be redesigned and the owner of each change. Show the required overlap and the preferred overlap as separate lines so the reader can see what is optional.

Close with the approved schedule, the review date, and the backup arrangement for work outside the overlap. If the evidence is incomplete, say so and name the additional collection needed. The output should be a schedule the team can defend, not a preference dressed up as a requirement. If the approved schedule differs from the current one, record the transition date and who owns the change.
