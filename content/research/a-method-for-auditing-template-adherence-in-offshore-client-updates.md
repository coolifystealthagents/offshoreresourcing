Recurring client updates are often governed by a template: a subject line, a status section, a list of open items, a next-step date, and a contact. The template exists so a client can scan the update and so the team does not forget a required field. Over time, workers adapt it, shorten it, or replace a section with a link. Some variations are harmless, while others remove the information a client needs. This method defines a small audit of template adherence. The audit should not reward a filled-in form with no useful content. A message can use every heading and still fail to tell the client what changed. The method therefore checks both the presence of the required sections and the quality of the content inside them. The output should improve the template and the instruction, not just produce a compliance score.

## Research question and decision supported

The question is how often recurring client updates follow the approved template and whether the deviations remove required information. The decision supported is whether the template, the instruction, or the review needs to change. A second decision is whether a specific update needs a correction before it reaches the client. The audit should also record whether the client asked for a different format, because an approved variation is not a failure.

Write the answer as a count by section and by deviation type. A single adherence rate hides the difference between a missing subject line and a missing next-step date. The section-level view tells the reviewer which part of the template is failing. Add the template version to the count so a change in the template does not look like a sudden drop in adherence.

## Definitions and scope

Define the template as the approved structure for the update, including the required fields and the order in which they appear. Define adherence as the presence of each required field with content that answers it. Define a deviation as a missing field, an empty field, a field with the wrong content, or an added section that changes the message. Exclude formatting differences that do not affect meaning.

Scope the audit to one client or one update type so the template is consistent. Include updates sent by every worker on the account, not only the newest hire. Exclude one-off incident messages that follow a different format, but list them so the boundary is clear. If the client has a separate escalation format, review it as its own template rather than forcing it into the main count.

## Sampling and collection

Sample a continuous period, such as four weeks, and include at least one week with a holiday or a staffing change. Collect the update text, the date, the sender, the client, and the template version. Do not edit the message before the review. If the update was sent in a thread, keep the thread context so a reply is not mistaken for the original.

Aim for enough messages to see a pattern, not a single example. If the volume is low, review every update in the period and say so. If the volume is high, sample by week and by sender. Record the sampling rule so the result is reproducible. A sample that misses a busy week can make adherence look better than it is.

## Scoring rubric

Score each required field on three levels: present and complete, present but incomplete, or missing. A complete field answers the question a client would ask, not just a heading with a placeholder. Score the overall update as ready, ready with a minor fix, or not ready. Use the same rubric for every message and have a second reviewer score a subset.

Keep the rubric short. A template with ten required fields and five quality levels will produce disagreement and fatigue. Three levels per field and three overall outcomes are enough to find the pattern. Record the exact evidence for each score so a later reviewer can see why. Apply the same rubric to every sample so the comparison is fair.

## Common deviations

Common deviations include a missing next-step date, a status that says in progress without an owner, a list of open items with no due date, and a summary that repeats the previous update. Another common deviation is a link to a dashboard with no explanation, which assumes the client has access and time to read it. The audit should name the deviation rather than only counting it. Record whether the client replied with a question, because a question is a sign that the update did not answer the need.

A deviation may be a deliberate improvement. If a worker replaced a long section with a clearer table, the template may be outdated. Review the deviation with the account owner before treating it as a failure. The audit should distinguish a better format from a missing requirement. Keep the decision and the reason with the sample.

## Causes and context

Look for the cause behind the pattern. A missing date may mean the worker does not know the date, the template does not require it, or the update was sent in a hurry. A missing owner may mean the queue does not assign one. The cause points to a different fix: a clearer field, a source of truth, or a review step.

Check the template version and the instruction the worker used. If the template changed and the worker used an old copy, the problem is distribution, not adherence. If the template has an ambiguous field, the problem is design. Record the version with each message so the review can separate the two. Note the version date beside every scored message.

## Reviewer agreement

Have a second reviewer score a sample independently and compare the results. Disagreement on a field usually means the rubric is unclear or the template field is ambiguous. Resolve the disagreement by writing a clearer definition, not by averaging the scores. A rubric that two reviewers cannot apply consistently will not help the team. Test it on five messages before scoring the full sample.

Check whether the reviewers are judging the same thing. One may be checking the presence of a heading while the other is checking the quality of the content. Define the level of the check before scoring. The audit is about whether the client gets the required information, not whether the message looks tidy. Write that scope at the top of the scoring sheet.

## Reporting and decisions

Report the period, the account, the number of updates, the adherence by field, the deviation types, and the examples. Include a short list of template or instruction fixes with owners and dates. Keep the client content redacted in the working report and store the original in the approved system. State the template version and the sampling rule in the report so another reviewer can repeat the count.

Decide whether the template is ready, needs a clarification, or needs a review step. If the same field fails repeatedly, change the template or the source record rather than adding another reminder. The goal is an update the client can scan and trust, not a form that is filled in for its own sake. If the fix requires a source record, name the system owner and the date the field will be available.

## Limitations and error

The sample may not represent the full year, and a short period may miss a quarterly update with a different format. The reviewer may also score a message as incomplete when the client already had the information from a call. Note these limits and avoid treating the score as a complete measure of communication quality. A short period can still be useful if the reader knows what it does not cover, so state the gap plainly.

The workers may change their format during the audit, which can make the sample look better than normal. Use a period before the audit is announced where possible, and compare with a later period. Describe the result as evidence for the sampled updates, not a verdict on the team. Keep a small before-and-after sample so the next review can see whether the format settled or drifted again.
