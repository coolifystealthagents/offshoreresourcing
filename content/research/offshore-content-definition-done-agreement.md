## Research question

When researchers, writers, editors, and publishers use the same completion criteria, do handoff disputes and returned work decline? A checklist may improve consistency, but it can also become ceremonial if its terms are vague or acceptance authority is hidden.

## Methodology and scope

Define observable criteria for brief fit, claim support, originality, internal links, metadata, accessibility, approval, and live verification. Give the same sample articles to reviewers and measure agreement before and after the definition is introduced. Track returns by reason, time to acceptance, later corrections, and criteria reviewers interpret differently.

Quality-management, internal-control, accessibility, and search guidance supports documented requirements and verification. It does not prove that a checklist causes better articles. The criteria and proposed agreement study are our synthesis for recurring offshore content work.

## Interpretation

High agreement matters only when the criteria reflect genuine reader and publication needs. Reviewers can agree on a weak standard. Pair agreement with source checks, rendered-route evidence, and correction outcomes. Separate a changed brief from failure to meet the accepted brief.

## Limitations

Reviewers may improve because they know they are observed. Article classes differ in risk, and low correction counts make outcome comparisons unstable. The study does not measure every aspect of usefulness or writing quality. Specialist claims still need appropriate expert review.

## Conclusion

A definition of done is valuable when each criterion has observable evidence and a named decision owner. Pilot it on representative articles, resolve ambiguous terms, and retain escalation rather than forcing uncertain work through a checkbox.
