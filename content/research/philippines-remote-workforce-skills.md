## The evidence boundary

National workforce data can describe context, not guarantee a hiring outcome. The Philippine Statistics Authority, ILOSTAT, and World Bank publish useful indicators on employment, education, and labor participation. Those indicators should inform sourcing hypotheses while candidate evidence remains role-specific.

## What to compare

| Data layer | Responsible use | Wrong inference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Labor supply | Understand market context | Assume every skill is available |
| Education | Shape screening questions | Treat credentials as performance |
| Occupation data | Clarify role families | Treat a title as a capability |
| Candidate work | Make the decision | Generalize from one sample |

The OECD warns that skills mismatch is common across labor markets, which reinforces the need to test the actual work. A staffing partner can add value by explaining how market context affects sourcing while keeping selection anchored to the agreed role.

## Practical use

Use public data to ask better questions: Which language and schedule requirements narrow the pool? Which adjacent occupations may contain transferable skills? Which qualifications need verification? Do not use averages to publish unsupported claims about quality, cost, or availability.

## Conclusion

Good workforce research is humble. It makes market assumptions visible and leaves the final judgment to verified, job-related evidence.
