## Decision in brief

Do not compare an employer-of-record arrangement, staffing service, and managed service as if they were three prices for the same product. First map who employs or engages the worker, directs daily work, supplies systems and equipment, checks quality, handles personal data, carries continuity duties, and owns the result. Then compare cost and contract terms against that same responsibility map.

Labels vary between providers and jurisdictions. This article does not determine employment status, tax treatment, lawful contracting, or regulatory obligations. Those conclusions depend on facts and require qualified Philippine and home-country advice. The purpose here is narrower: make retained work and unclear ownership visible before a buyer selects a structure.

## Define the outcome and the unit being bought

Describe the business need without naming a structure. For example: maintain an interview schedule, prepare weekly workforce records, cover a customer queue during stated hours, or deliver an approved set of reconciliations. Identify volume, variation, systems, sensitive data, decision rights, quality evidence, and service window.

Next state the unit of purchase. A named full-time worker, a pool of capacity, a covered queue, and a defined output are different units. If a proposal combines them, separate the elements. Buyers frequently compare a salary-like monthly amount with a bundled service fee even though the second includes work the first leaves internal.

Document constraints such as required accounts, work location, manager overlap, customer commitments, professional approvals, and data residency. A structure that cannot satisfy a mandatory condition should not survive merely because its estimate is lower.

## Treat provider labels as hypotheses

An employer-of-record provider commonly describes itself as the local employer while the client directs much of the worker's daily activity. A staffing provider may recruit and employ assigned personnel while offering varying levels of HR and operational support. A managed-service provider may take responsibility for a process or result using its own management and staffing choices. Actual contracts and conduct may differ.

The same provider may offer several models. Marketing names do not settle which party controls work, bears risk, or owes a legal duty. Ask for the contracting entity, employing or engaging entity, service description, and responsibility schedule. Have advisers test the written arrangement against the intended practice.

Independent contracting is not an automatic fourth price column. Worker classification is fact specific. Direction, integration, economic reality, and local rules may matter. Do not use a contract label to avoid the analysis.

## Build the responsibility map

Use four statuses for each activity: responsible for doing the work, accountable for the final decision, consulted, and informed. Add an evidence field and an exception owner. Avoid assigning shared accountability, which often means nobody knows who must decide.

| Activity | Questions to settle | Evidence to request |
|---|---|---|
| Role design | Who defines tasks, hours, outputs, and stop rules? | Approved role brief and change route |
| Recruitment | Who sources, screens, assesses, and selects? | Selection workflow and decision record |
| Engagement | Who signs with and pays the worker? | Contracting and employing-entity statement |
| Daily direction | Who prioritizes work and answers questions? | Operating map and named manager |
| Quality | Who defines acceptance and handles rework? | Sample report and review method |
| Access | Who approves, provisions, reviews, and removes it? | Access matrix and removal evidence |
| Equipment | Who owns, configures, repairs, and recovers devices? | Asset and support procedure |
| Data | Who decides purpose and means, processes data, and handles incidents? | Privacy roles and processing terms |
| Continuity | Who covers leave, absence, outage, and replacement? | Coverage and transition plan |
| Exit | Who returns data, closes accounts, transfers work, and recovers assets? | Exit schedule and acceptance record |

The table is an operating aid, not a legal classification test. Give the legal team the same factual map so their review reflects how work is expected to run.

## Separate employment administration from supervision

Payroll, statutory administration, benefits, leave records, and employment documentation may sit with one party while task direction and acceptance sit with another. Write both layers. A buyer should know whom the worker contacts about an employment matter and whom the worker contacts about a blocked customer task.

The Philippine Labor Code and Department of Labor resources provide the primary legal context, but a general source cannot decide a particular arrangement. Ask advisers to confirm obligations for the employing entity, client, and any contractor or subcontractor. Record what evidence the buyer is entitled to receive without collecting unnecessary worker data.

Daily supervision deserves its own capacity estimate. If the buyer supplies priorities, training, approvals, feedback, and exception decisions, include that manager time in the comparison. A provider relationship does not remove the buyer's need to own business policy, system authority, and consequential decisions.

## Map privacy and security roles

List each category of data the role needs: candidate details, employee records, customer messages, financial documents, credentials, or operational logs. For each one, identify purpose, authorized users, system, location, retention rule, and incident route. Minimize access before negotiating controls for data that the role does not need.

The Philippine Data Privacy Act implementing rules distinguish accountability and require appropriate safeguards. Contract terms should identify relevant parties and assistance duties, but wording alone is insufficient. Map who configures accounts, manages devices, monitors events, approves exports, responds to rights requests, reports incidents, and proves deletion or return.

Use NIST frameworks as structured question sets. They do not certify that a structure is safe. A managed service may control more of the technical environment; an EOR arrangement may leave most systems with the buyer. Verify the actual boundary.

## Compare management and quality control

For a named-worker model, the buyer may set the daily queue and inspect individual work. For a managed outcome, the provider may allocate staff and run internal review while the buyer accepts service results. Some arrangements sit between these points.

Define the acceptance unit and evidence. If the service is interview scheduling, measure complete, accurate scheduling records and exceptions, not merely messages sent. If the service is workforce reporting, define source records, reconciliation, review date, and approval. The party doing quality review needs access to the authoritative record and authority to return work.

Decide who changes instructions. Informal direction from several buyer stakeholders can undermine a managed process and create conflicting priorities. Use one approved channel, effective dates, and a route for urgent exceptions.

## Price the retained responsibilities

Build a twelve-month view with provider charges, worker-related costs where visible and relevant, setup, equipment, software, security, recruitment, onboarding, management, quality review, coverage, replacement, foreign exchange, and exit. Separate cash from internal time. Keep uncertain items as ranges.

An EOR fee may cover employment administration but not daily management. A staffing fee may include recruitment and replacement but leave process design with the buyer. A managed-service price may include operational supervision and quality control while restricting direct assignment of individuals. Confirm each inclusion in the proposal and contract.

Do not treat a provider's fee as waste without identifying the responsibility it funds. Conversely, do not treat a bundled label as proof that a responsibility is included. The map should show the owner and the acceptance evidence.

## Test exceptions and change

Walk through a missed shift, access failure, quality defect, complaint, security concern, urgent reprioritization, role expansion, and worker departure. For each case, ask who notices, who contains the issue, who communicates, who decides, what record is kept, and when the buyer can intervene.

Then test a change in volume or hours. A named-worker arrangement may need a schedule or employment change. A managed service may use a volume band or change request. The contract should describe notice, pricing, feasibility, and effect on service commitments.

An exception path is especially important across time zones. State what can wait, what must stop, and which backup owner can decide when the primary manager is unavailable.

## Pilot the chosen boundary

Run a bounded pilot using the responsibility map as the test plan. Start with synthetic data and limited access. Include a normal item, a missing input, a return for correction, and a handoff. Record who actually performs each step and compare that record with the promised model.

Measure output acceptance, unresolved exceptions, buyer review time, provider management actions, access events, and changes to instructions. A successful output can still expose an unsustainable dependency on one buyer manager. A slow result can reveal a missing input rather than insufficient staff.

At the review date, update the map to reflect practice. Proceed only if material responsibilities have named owners, evidence, and workable escalation. Attach the final map to governance records or the contract where appropriate.

## Limitations

Provider categories are not standardized, and legal consequences cannot be inferred from this operational model. Public rules can change. Contracts, tax positions, regulated activities, intellectual property, benefits, and worker rights require specialist advice.

A pilot is a small sample and may not show peak demand, long absence, turnover, or a serious incident. Revisit the map when scope, provider entities, systems, location, volume, or supervision changes.

## Buyer conclusion

The useful comparison is not EOR versus staffing versus managed service in the abstract. It is a comparison of who does what for a defined work lane, what the buyer retains, how each responsibility is evidenced, and what happens when normal work breaks. Once that is visible, price and risk can be compared on the same basis.
