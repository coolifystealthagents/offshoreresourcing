## Research question

Which clarification events in recurring offshore article work could have been prevented by a more complete brief? The useful question is not whether writers ask questions. Good questions often prevent unsupported claims. The question is whether the commissioning record omitted a decision the owner already could have made.

## Methodology and scope

Select comparable assignments and preserve each original brief. Record clarification questions, the missing field, response time, search or draft work affected, and whether the answer changed audience, scope, claim, or evidence requirements. Two reviewers should classify a sample independently. The assignment is the unit of analysis.

Research-planning and content-design guidance supports explicit questions, audience needs, and documented assumptions. No cited source measures this site's offshore brief burden. The classification and proposed application are our inference.

## Interpretation

Distinguish legitimate discovery from preventable ambiguity. Conflicting evidence discovered during research is not necessarily a bad brief. Missing jurisdiction, intended reader, approval owner, or claim threshold often is. Count questions alongside their material effect rather than rewarding silence.

## Limitations

Chat records may miss verbal clarifications. Writers differ in how readily they surface uncertainty. Reviewer classification can be subjective, and a changed business priority can resemble an incomplete original brief. The study cannot establish a universal cost per question.

## Conclusion

Use the result to repair repeatable input fields, not to discourage questions. A successful brief reduces owner-resolvable ambiguity while preserving escalation for evidence conflicts and newly discovered limits.
