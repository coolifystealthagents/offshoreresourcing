## Finding

Accessibility is a capability, not a final visual preference. A remote content specialist may need to understand headings, link purpose, text alternatives, contrast, keyboard use, and plain language. Those skills affect whether an article can be used by more people and maintained correctly over time.

## What to assess

| Capability | Observable evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Structure | Correct heading hierarchy in a sample |
| Language | Clear labels and descriptive links |
| Media awareness | Useful alternative text decisions |
| Verification | Ability to explain a limitation |

WCAG 2.2 provides testable success criteria, while W3C’s authoring guidance explains how content decisions affect accessibility. The U.S. Access Board and UK Government Service Manual add practical context. Together they support role-specific assessment instead of an unsupported claim that a candidate is “accessibility aware.”

## Resourcing implication

If accessibility matters to the site, include it in the role brief and sample rubric. A staffing partner should be able to identify whether a candidate has practical experience, formal training, or only general familiarity. Those are different evidence levels.

## Conclusion

Accessibility belongs at the capability-definition stage because the person shaping content can prevent barriers before they become expensive to correct.
