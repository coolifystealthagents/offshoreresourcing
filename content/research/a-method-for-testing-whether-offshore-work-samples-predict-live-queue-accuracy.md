Work samples are widely used in offshore hiring because they feel more job-relevant than an interview, but a sample is only useful if it predicts how someone performs on the live queue. A candidate can produce a careful exercise answer with unlimited context and then struggle with incomplete records, time pressure, and ambiguous ownership. This report sets out a method for comparing sample scores with later live accuracy without overstating what a small cohort can prove. The goal is a defensible hiring signal, not a guarantee of performance.

## Research question and decision supported

The question is whether scores on a standardized work sample are associated with accuracy on the live queue during the first review period. The decision supported is whether to keep, revise, or retire the exercise as a hiring signal. A second decision is whether the sample should weight a particular skill, such as exception recognition or record hygiene, more heavily. The analysis should be planned before the cohort is hired so the outcome and the scoring rules are not chosen after the results are known. It should also define the minimum cohort size that would make the result worth acting on. A handful of hires can suggest a direction but cannot establish a stable relationship.

## Define the predictor and the outcome

The predictor is the work-sample score, broken into its rubric dimensions rather than only a total. Keep the dimension scores because a sample may predict record accuracy but not escalation judgment. Record the reviewer, the rubric version, and the time limit so the predictor is reproducible. The outcome is live accuracy measured with the same acceptance standard used in normal review. Use a fixed review period, a defined sample of live items, and a consistent defect definition. If possible, have the live reviewer score the work without seeing the candidate's sample score, so the outcome is not biased by the hiring decision.

## Sample design and cohort tracking

Track every person hired into the same role family during the study period, not only the strong or weak performers. Record the hire date, the start date, the training path, and the first live queue assignment. Exclude people whose role changed before the live sample for a reason unrelated to performance, and state the exclusion rule in advance. Collect the live sample after the person has completed the same onboarding checkpoint, so the comparison is not distorted by different start dates. If the onboarding differs by shift or client, record that difference and analyze it separately rather than pooling everyone into one average.

## Keep scoring independent

Score the work samples before the person starts, using two reviewers on a subset to check consistency. Store the scores separately from the live review record so the later reviewer cannot anchor on them. If the same manager conducts the interview, the sample review, and the live review, the analysis carries a higher risk of bias. Use a rubric with observable anchors rather than a single global impression. For example, score whether the candidate found the missing approval, cited the relevant rule, and recorded the next action. A total score can hide a candidate who is strong on one dimension and weak on another, which is exactly the pattern a predictive test should reveal.

## Analyze the relationship cautiously

With a small cohort, start with a scatter plot and a rank comparison rather than a complex model. Show each person's sample score and live accuracy, and describe the pattern in plain language. If the cohort is large enough, calculate a correlation and a confidence interval, but do not present a single coefficient as proof of causation. Check for range restriction. If the hiring process already rejected low sample scores, the remaining cohort may show little variation, which makes the relationship look weaker than it is. Report the range and the number of candidates who were not hired, because the sample can only describe the people who entered the role.

## Threats to validity

The biggest threat is a change in the live work between the sample and the outcome period. A new client, system, or template can make the sample task easier or harder than the live queue. Record those changes and avoid comparing cohorts hired under substantially different conditions. A second threat is training quality. Two people with the same sample score may receive different coaching, different queue assignments, or different access. The outcome may reflect the onboarding rather than the predictive value of the exercise. A third threat is reviewer drift, where the live reviewer gradually changes the standard during the period.

## Decision rules for keeping or changing the sample

Decide in advance what result would lead to keeping the exercise, revising a dimension, or retiring it. For example, keep the exercise if higher sample scores consistently align with higher live accuracy across two cohorts, and revise it if one dimension predicts nothing. Do not keep an exercise merely because it is familiar or because candidates say they liked it. If the sample does not predict live accuracy, examine whether the exercise tests the wrong task or whether the live measure is too noisy. A poorly designed live sample can hide a useful hiring signal. Before retiring the exercise, check the rubric, the scoring consistency, and the outcome definition.

## Limitations

This method cannot prove that the work sample causes better hiring outcomes, and a small cohort may produce an unstable result. It also cannot capture every relevant skill, especially those that develop after months in the role. Use the result as one input to the hiring process, alongside structured interviews and reference checks. Report the cohort size, the period, the rubric version, the outcome definition, and the exclusions. Keep the data private and avoid publishing individual scores. Repeat the analysis when the role, the queue, or the sample changes so the hiring signal is based on current work rather than an old exercise.

## Designing the live outcome measure

The live outcome should use the same acceptance standard as normal quality review, not a special test that candidates cannot see. Define the sample size, the review period, and the defect categories before the first hire starts. Use a reviewer who did not score the work sample, or at least keep the sample score hidden during the live review. Record the queue type and the difficulty of the sampled items. An outcome measure that changes with each cohort cannot support a comparison.

## When the sample and the queue test different skills

A work sample may test careful reading while the live queue rewards speed and navigation across several systems. Compare the task steps in the exercise with the steps in a normal live item and list the differences. If the exercise gives unlimited time or perfect context, it may not predict performance under incomplete records. Add one realistic constraint to the sample rather than making the whole exercise harder. The goal is alignment with the actual work, not a more elaborate test.

## Handling small cohorts honestly

With five or six hires, a single strong or weak performer can create a pattern that looks meaningful but is not stable. Report the individual pairs and the range, and describe the result as a signal rather than a conclusion. Ask the reviewers whether the pattern matches their experience with the cohort. If the result is used to change the hiring process, start with a small revision and track the next cohort. Avoid presenting a correlation without the sample size and the uncertainty.

## Using the result in a hiring debrief

The predictive check belongs in the hiring process review, not in an individual candidate's feedback. Discuss whether the exercise tested the right skills and whether the rubric was clear enough to score consistently. If a dimension did not predict anything, consider whether it is still worth measuring for development rather than selection. Keep the hiring decision record separate from the validation analysis. The purpose is to improve the process for future candidates, not to relitigate a past hire.
