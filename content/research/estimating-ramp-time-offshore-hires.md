Ramp time is the period between a new hire's start date and the point when their work can be handled without close review. Teams often estimate this period from memory or from a training plan, which can make the first month look smoother than it was. A work-record method uses actual queue entries, review decisions, and correction notes to show when independence increased. The result is a range with stated assumptions, not a promise about any individual. Compare [offshore staffing services](/services) with the [research library](/research) when you translate this method into a review plan.

The method is useful for planning review capacity, setting client expectations, and deciding when to add a second hire. It is not a performance rating. A new hire may take longer because the work is complex, the access was late, or the source records are poor. The estimate should help managers separate those causes rather than label the person as slow.

## Research question and decision supported

The question is how long a new offshore hire needed, in a defined role and period, to produce work that a reviewer accepted without a substantive correction. The decision supported is how much review capacity to reserve in the first weeks and when to expand the hire's scope. A second decision is whether the onboarding materials or the access setup need repair before the next hire starts. Report the threshold and the observation window beside the result so the reader can judge comparability.

The estimate should be reported as a range, because the exact point of independence is a judgment. Define the threshold in advance, such as three consecutive accepted items or a stated correction rate over a defined volume. Report the threshold and the evidence behind it. A vague claim that the person was productive after two weeks is not a usable estimate.

## Population and unit of analysis

Define the population as new hires who started in the role during the window and whose work records are available. Include hires who left before reaching the threshold, because excluding them would overstate the success rate. Exclude contractors who transferred from another team with the same systems and tasks, unless the transfer is part of the question. Report the exclusions.

The unit is one new hire, with repeated work items nested inside that hire. Do not treat each work item as an independent ramp observation, because the items share the same person and the same learning curve. If the sample has only a few hires, report the individual trajectories rather than a single average. A small sample can still be useful if the uncertainty is visible.

## Definitions and measures

Define the start date as the first day the hire could access the queue and begin work, not the offer date or the training date. Define the review outcome as the reviewer's recorded decision: accepted, accepted with minor correction, returned for substantive correction, or escalated. Define independence as the first sustained period that meets the agreed threshold. Record any later regression, because ramp is not always a straight line.

Measure the number of items completed, the number accepted without correction, the number returned, and the time to each outcome. Report the first accepted item, the first sustained acceptance, and the correction rate by week. Include the review hours spent per item if the data exists, because review capacity is often the real constraint. Do not reduce the result to a single average week.

## Evidence and data dictionary

The data dictionary should include the hire identifier, the start date, the role, the queue, the reviewer, the item identifier, the review date, the outcome, the correction reason, and the evidence link. Add fields for access start date, training completion, and any change in scope. These fields let the reviewer separate a learning curve from a setup delay. Add a note for any review that was completed after a discussion, because a changed decision can alter the ramp date.

Capture the review outcome as it was recorded at the time, not as it is reconstructed later. If the reviewer changed a decision after discussion, record the change and the reason. Preserve the correction notes because they show what the hire had to learn. A bare accepted or rejected flag is not enough to explain the ramp.

## Sampling and collection

Select a window that covers the full onboarding period and at least two weeks after the expected threshold. Include the first items even if they were later reworked. Collect the review records in one pass and keep the raw export separate from the analysis. Record the queue volume during the period, because a quiet queue can make the ramp look faster than it was.

Ask the reviewer to confirm the threshold and the review standard before the analysis. If the threshold changed during the period, split the analysis at the change. Do not compare hires who worked under different standards without noting the difference. The collection should be light enough that it does not change the review behavior it is trying to measure.

## Reviewer independence and agreement

Have a second reviewer independently classify a subset of items as accepted, minor correction, or substantive correction. Compare the classifications and discuss the differences. A disagreement may show that the correction categories are unclear or that reviewers apply different standards. Record the agreed definitions before extending the analysis.

Agreement does not prove that the threshold is right. Two reviewers can agree that an item was accepted while disagreeing about whether it was truly independent. Check the evidence behind the acceptance decision. If the reviewer fixed the item before accepting it, the hire may not have reached independence even though the record says accepted.

## What the evidence can support

The evidence can support a review-capacity plan, a scope-expansion decision, and a comparison between onboarding approaches. It can show whether the first week is dominated by access problems or by learning the work. It can also show whether corrections cluster around a specific task, which points to a training gap rather than a general capability issue. A short table of hires with their individual dates is more honest than a single average when the sample is small.

The evidence cannot predict how a future hire will perform. It describes a small number of past hires under specific conditions. It also cannot prove that a training program caused faster ramp unless the program is compared with a suitable baseline. Report the conditions and the uncertainty so the reader does not overgeneralize.

## Limitations and error

Reviewer leniency is a major limitation. A reviewer who wants the hire to succeed may accept work that a client would reject. A reviewer who is overloaded may accept work without checking. Use a sample of client or independent reviews to calibrate the acceptance standard. If those reviews are not available, state the limitation.

The start date can also distort the estimate. If access was delayed by a week, the learning curve starts later than the calendar suggests. Record the access start separately. Another limitation is the queue mix: a hire who starts during a quiet period may look independent sooner because the difficult work has not arrived yet.

## Sensitivity and alternatives

Rerun the estimate with a stricter threshold, such as five consecutive accepted items, and with a looser one. If the conclusion changes, report the range rather than a single date. Rerun with the first week excluded to see whether setup problems are driving the result. Rerun with the most complex work type excluded to see whether the mix explains the difference.

Consider alternatives before concluding that the hire needed more time. The delay may come from missing access, unclear instructions, an unusually difficult queue, or a reviewer bottleneck. Each has a different response. Name the alternative and the evidence that would distinguish it. The goal is a better onboarding design, not a judgment about the person.

## Privacy, access, and retention

Use hire identifiers rather than names in the working file. Keep the crosswalk in a restricted location. Do not copy performance notes that are not needed for the analysis. If the sample includes client data, aggregate the outcomes before sharing. The analysis should follow the same access rules as the review records.

Set a retention period and remove the raw export when the analysis is closed. Keep the final report and the method notes for the period required by the client agreement. Log access to the working file. Ramp analysis touches personnel data, so the privacy boundary should be explicit.

## Reporting template

Report the window, the hires included, the threshold, the review standard, and the exclusions. Present the individual trajectories and the range, not only the average. Include the access start date and the queue volume. State the limitations and the calibration source.

Close with the review-capacity estimate, the onboarding changes, the owner, and the next review date. If the sample is too small, say so and describe what a larger sample would require. A ramp estimate is useful when it helps the next hire start better. That next review should use the same threshold so the comparison remains meaningful.
