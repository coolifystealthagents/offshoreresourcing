A weekly quality review is often planned as a single calendar block, but the real cost includes preparing the sample, reading the work, writing corrections, checking resubmissions, and following up on repeated defects. When those steps are not measured, a review that looks affordable in a planning meeting can consume hours that the manager does not have. This report defines a practical way to measure the full review cycle and compare it with available capacity. The result is a planning estimate for one team and one period, not a universal benchmark.

## Research question and decision supported

The question is how many manager minutes a weekly quality review consumes from sample selection through closure of the corrections. The decision supported is whether the current sample size and cadence fit the manager's protected review time. A second decision is whether some review steps can be delegated or automated without weakening the control. Measure the full cycle rather than only the time spent reading. A review that takes ten minutes to read but twenty minutes to explain and recheck is a thirty-minute activity. The estimate should also separate recurring work from one-off work such as rewriting a template after a pattern of defects.

## Define the time categories

Use five categories: sample selection, first-pass review, correction writing, resubmission check, and follow-up or coaching. Sample selection includes pulling the items and confirming that they represent the week's work. First-pass review includes opening the record, reading the output, and comparing it with the standard. Correction writing includes the note and any evidence gathering needed to make the note specific. Resubmission check includes the second review of corrected work. Follow-up includes a conversation, a template change, or a message to the client when the defect has a cause outside the queue. Keep the categories stable across weeks so the totals are comparable.

## Select a representative review cycle

Measure at least two consecutive weekly cycles, including one ordinary week and one with a known exception or a new task type. If the review happens monthly, measure the full monthly cycle instead and note the different cadence. Record the number of items in the sample, the number of reviewers, and the request types covered. Do not choose only a quiet week because it is easier to observe. The review cost rises with defects, questions, and new work, and those are precisely the weeks that affect capacity. If a week is unusual, keep it in the data and label the reason rather than excluding it silently.

## Use a low-friction measurement protocol

Ask the reviewer to note the start and end time for each category on a simple sheet or a shared form. A timer is more accurate than a retrospective estimate, but it can interrupt the work if it requires constant switching. A timestamp at the start and end of each category is usually enough for a weekly review. Have a second person observe one cycle to check that the categories are being applied consistently. The observer should note waiting time separately, because a reviewer who waits for access or for a worker's reply is not actively reviewing. Waiting time still consumes the calendar, so report it alongside the active minutes rather than hiding it.

## Build the capacity model

Add the category totals to get the review cycle cost, then divide by the number of items to get the average minutes per item. Report the median and the range as well, because one complex item can dominate a small sample. Multiply the per-item figure by the planned weekly sample to estimate the recurring load. Compare the recurring load with the manager's protected review hours, not with total working hours. Protected hours are the blocks that are not already committed to meetings, one-to-ones, and other operating work. If the review requires more time than the protected block, the sample size, the cadence, or the reviewer assignment needs to change.

## Interpret the result without blaming the reviewer

A high review time may mean the sample is too large, the standard is hard to apply, or the underlying work is producing many defects. It does not automatically mean the manager is slow. Separate the time spent finding defects from the time spent explaining them, because the second cost often points to an instruction problem rather than an individual performance problem. If the review consistently finds few defects but still takes a long time, check whether the sample is too broad or the record is hard to navigate. If it finds many defects, the review is doing useful work and the cost may be justified. The decision should depend on the risk being controlled, not on a target number of minutes.

## Reduce cost without hiding risk

The safest reduction is to narrow the sample to the work with the highest consequence and use a lighter check for stable, low-risk work. A second option is to train a peer reviewer for routine items and reserve the manager for exceptions and calibration. A third is to improve the template so the reviewer does not have to search for the standard on every item. Do not reduce the sample simply to hit a time target. If the review disappears, defects may surface later as customer complaints or rework, which is a more expensive form of detection. Keep a small exception-focused sample even when routine review is delegated, and record the reason for any change in coverage.

## Limitations and reporting

Self-reported timestamps can be rounded, and the presence of an observer can change the pace of work. The estimate also reflects the selected weeks and may not hold during a peak season or a system migration. Report the period, sample size, categories, and any concurrent changes so the reader can judge how far the result generalizes. Use the result as a planning input, not as a performance rating for the reviewer. Share the category totals and the capacity comparison with the people who set the review cadence. Repeat the measurement after a material change and keep the old result so the team can see whether the change actually reduced effort.

## The hidden cost of review context switching

A review spread across five short gaps can cost more than the same review done in one block. Each switch requires reopening the standard, remembering the prior item, and finding the place in the queue again. Measure the number of interruptions in a review cycle and the time needed to resume after each one. If the manager is pulled into urgent requests during the block, the review may never reach a stable pace. Protect a longer block for the first-pass review and batch the corrections separately.

## Choosing a sample size from risk

A fixed percentage is simple, but it can over-sample stable work and under-sample new or high-consequence tasks. List the task types by consequence and assign a higher review rate to new, payment-adjacent, or customer-facing work. Keep a small random sample of stable work so a new defect pattern can still surface. The total should fit the protected hours rather than an abstract quality target. Review the allocation when the task mix changes.

## What to record when the review is interrupted

An interrupted review can leave an item half-checked and a correction half-written. Record the interruption, the item, and the point where the reviewer stopped so the next session can resume without redoing the whole check. If interruptions are frequent, the review log should show the lost time separately from the active review time. This prevents the capacity estimate from treating a fragmented hour as a full hour of review. It also helps explain why a review took longer than the item count suggests.

## Reporting review capacity to the client

The client does not need the manager's calendar, but it does need to know that review capacity is finite. Report the sample size, the review cadence, the main defect categories, and the hours reserved for review. When a new request would exceed the reserved capacity, show the trade-off between sample size and volume. A short capacity note is easier to discuss than a surprise delay. Update it when the scope or the review standard changes.
