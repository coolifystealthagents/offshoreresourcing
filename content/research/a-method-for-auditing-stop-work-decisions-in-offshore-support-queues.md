A stop-work decision is the moment a worker pauses an item because the next action is unsafe, unauthorized, or dependent on information that is missing. Good stops prevent errors and protect customers, but unnecessary stops create delay and hide a queue that lacks clear instructions. An audit can distinguish the two only if it examines the evidence available at the moment of the decision, not the outcome with hindsight. This report defines a reproducible audit method for those decisions.

## Research question and decision supported

The question is whether each stop-work decision was supported by the evidence and the authority rules that applied when the worker paused the item. The decision supported is whether the queue needs clearer stop conditions, better source records, or a faster approval path. A second decision is whether the team is stopping too often because the instructions are ambiguous or too rarely because workers are pushing through uncertainty. The audit should not ask whether the final outcome happened to be acceptable. A worker can make a correct stop and still see the item close successfully after a manager resolves it. Judge the decision against the information and rules available at the time, and record any later change separately.

## Define a stop-work decision

A stop-work decision occurs when the worker does not complete the next routine action and records a pause, an escalation, or a request for information. Include formal holds and informal pauses that were later recorded, because the informal ones often reveal the real friction. Exclude breaks, scheduled meetings, and ordinary waiting for a downstream system that the worker cannot influence. Record the reason in the worker's own words, the rule or threshold that triggered the stop, the evidence the worker inspected, and the person who could release the item. If the worker did not record a reason, the audit should mark the evidence as missing rather than reconstructing a reason from the outcome. That distinction matters for improving the process.

## Set the evidence standard

The standard should come from the written role instructions and approval matrix. A valid stop should identify the condition, show the record that supports it, and name the authority that can resolve it. If the condition is a missing document, the stop should name the document and the request that was sent. If the condition is an unauthorized action, the stop should cite the rule. Some stops are precautionary and have no specific rule because the situation is new. In those cases, the evidence standard is whether the worker described the uncertainty clearly and routed it to the right person. The audit should have a separate category for a justified precaution, so the team does not treat every novel situation as a rule violation.

## Sample design

Sample from the items that were paused or escalated during the period, not from the full queue. Stratify by task type, shift, and reason code where the volume allows. Include items that were paused for less than an hour and items that waited more than a day, because short stops and long stops may have different causes. Select a comparison group of items that were completed without a stop, especially items of the same type. This helps the auditor see whether the stop condition is consistently applied. A task that one worker stops and another completes may reveal an unclear rule rather than a difference in caution.

## Audit steps

For each sampled item, read the worker's note, the source record, the instruction that applied, and the approval path. Ask four questions: was the condition real, was it within the worker's authority to resolve, was the right person asked, and was the pause recorded at the time? Code each answer with a short evidence note and a link to the record. Have a second reviewer independently audit a subset and compare the codes. Discuss disagreements about whether a rule was clear enough to apply. If the reviewers cannot agree, the instruction is probably ambiguous, which is itself a finding. Keep the audit sheet separate from the performance review so the worker is not penalized for surfacing a process gap.

## Classify the outcomes

Use four categories: justified stop, justified precaution, unnecessary stop, and unclear instruction. A justified stop has a real condition and a correct authority path. A justified precaution has a novel situation that was handled carefully. An unnecessary stop has no condition or ignores a rule that allowed the worker to proceed. An unclear instruction means the evidence does not let the auditor decide, and the repair belongs with the instruction owner. Report the categories separately and include examples with sensitive details removed. A high unnecessary-stop rate may indicate over-caution or a training gap, while a high unclear-instruction rate points to documentation. A high justified-stop rate may mean the queue is working as designed, not that the team is slow.

## Reporting and follow-up

Report the period, sample size, task types, category counts, and the most common stop conditions. Add the time each stop added to the item and the owner who released it. Recommend specific changes such as a clearer threshold, a new intake field, a faster approver, or a training example. Assign an owner and a review date to each recommendation. Repeat the audit after the change and compare the same categories. If justified stops fall but errors rise, the new rule may be too permissive. If unnecessary stops fall without an error increase, the instruction probably improved. Keep the audit evidence for the review period and avoid publishing individual worker results.

## Limitations

The audit depends on records that may be incomplete, and a worker may pause an item without writing a note. A missing note is an evidence gap, not proof that the stop was unjustified. The auditor should also account for the worker's experience and the information visible on the screen at the time. Stop-work decisions are context-dependent, and a small sample may not represent rare high-risk cases. Add a targeted review of any incident or near miss from the period. State the sample, the rules version, and the reviewer agreement in the report so the reader can judge how much confidence the findings support.

## Separating a stop from a slow handoff

A worker may pause an item correctly and then wait too long for the release, which is a different problem from an unnecessary stop. Code the stop decision separately from the release time and the approver's response. If the decision was right but the release was slow, the repair belongs in the approval path. If the decision was unnecessary, the repair belongs in the instruction or the training. Combining both into one delay measure hides which part to fix.

## Auditing the release path, not only the pause

For each justified stop, record who released the item, how long it waited, and what evidence the approver used. A stop that never reaches the right person is not a complete control, even if the worker was correct to pause. Check whether the approver had a backup and whether the queue recorded the handoff. If the release path depends on one manager, the audit should flag that dependency. The control includes the return to work, not only the pause.

## Using near misses as audit evidence

A near miss is an item that could have caused harm but was caught before it did. These cases are valuable because they show where the stop condition almost failed without the cost of an actual incident. Include near misses in the sample and code the point where the work was caught. Ask whether the catch was a written rule, a colleague's review, or luck. A pattern of lucky catches is a sign that the stop conditions need to be clearer.

## Feeding findings back without punishing caution

If workers believe that every stop will be counted against them, they will push through uncertainty and hide near misses. Present the audit as a review of the rule and the release path, not a list of individual mistakes. Share aggregate findings and a few anonymized examples, and invite the team to challenge the evidence standard. Correct the instruction when the audit finds ambiguity. The goal is more accurate stops, not fewer stops at any cost.
