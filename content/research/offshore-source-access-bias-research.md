## Research question

When researchers cannot access a preferred source, does the final article narrow its claim transparently or drift toward whatever evidence is easiest to retrieve? Availability bias in this setting is an operating risk: accessible material may be mistaken for representative evidence.

## Evidence scope and method

This desk review draws on evidence-synthesis and information-literacy guidance. It does not measure the prevalence of access bias at Offshore Resourcing. A prospective source log is needed.

Before searching, state each material question and the source types that could answer it. Log candidate source, publisher, date, relevance, access result, substitute, and resulting claim change. Compare the planned evidence mix with the cited mix. Review excluded sources rather than recording only successful searches. A blocked source is not automatically superior, and an open source is not automatically weak; authority, method, currency, and fit require separate judgments.

## Operating boundaries

The researcher may find alternatives and recommend narrower wording. The research editor decides whether evidence is sufficient, whether a limitation belongs in the article, and whether the topic should be held. Credentials must not be shared to bypass access controls, and summaries should not be cited as if the underlying study was inspected.

## Limitations

The planned evidence mix reflects editorial judgment and may itself be incomplete. Access changes by geography, institution, and time. Citation counts do not measure evidence quality. Guidance for systematic reviews is more rigorous than a commercial desk review, so its principles should be scaled honestly rather than used to imply a formal review.

## Conclusion

Source access can shape an article silently. A predeclared question list and an exclusion log make that influence inspectable. When access removes essential evidence, narrow or hold the claim instead of filling the gap with convenient secondary material.
