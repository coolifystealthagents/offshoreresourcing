## Research question and scope

What evidence is needed when an editorial rubric changes while articles are in progress? This review addresses version identification, reviewer calibration, and interpretation of results. It does not recommend a universal rubric or a performance-ranking system.

## Methodology

We reviewed primary measurement and quality guidance on documented procedures, inter-rater reliability, and change control. We translated those principles into minimum records for a distributed editorial team. We did not score articles or estimate agreement among OffshoreResourcing.com reviewers.

## Findings

Each review result should identify the rubric version, reviewer, review date, article state, and any rule applied outside the standard. A version note should explain the changed criterion, effective date, approval owner, and treatment of work already underway.

Scores from different versions may not be directly comparable. Even with one version, agreement depends on clear definitions and representative examples. Calibration should focus on disputed classifications and reasoning, not on forcing identical wording from every reviewer.

## Inference boundaries

Agreement is not the same as correctness. Reviewers can consistently apply a weak rule, while sensible disagreement may reveal an ambiguous criterion. A score change after a rubric revision cannot be attributed to writer quality unless the effects of the version and article mix are addressed.

## Limitations and practical use

This synthesis does not establish an acceptable reliability threshold for the company. That choice depends on the decision and cost of an error. Start by versioning the current rubric, double-reviewing a small varied sample, and discussing disagreements before changing targets.
