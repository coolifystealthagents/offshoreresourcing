When an onshore manager leaves, the visible work usually continues while the invisible knowledge quietly disappears. New offshore hires may still be onboarded, but the exceptions, client preferences, and unwritten approvals that made the process work may no longer be available. A departure is therefore a chance to measure how much onboarding knowledge is documented, how much is shared, and how much exists only in one person's head. This report describes a coverage method that can be used before the last day.

## Research question and decision supported

The question is what share of the onboarding decisions and context held by a departing manager is available to a receiving owner and documented well enough to be used without that manager. The decision supported is where to spend the remaining handover time and which knowledge gaps require a temporary support arrangement. A second decision is whether onboarding can continue at the current volume during the transition. The method does not try to capture everything a manager knows. It focuses on the knowledge that changes what a new hire is told, approved for, or warned about.

## Define onboarding knowledge

Onboarding knowledge includes the sequence of tasks, the acceptance standard for early work, the access a new hire needs, the client-specific preferences, the common mistakes, and the people who can answer questions. It also includes the informal rules about when to wait, when to ask, and when to proceed. Separate this from general role knowledge that is already written in a public guide. The target is the knowledge that a new hire needs in the first thirty to sixty days. Write the scope down so the inventory does not expand into every fact the manager has accumulated over years.

Separate knowledge that affects a decision from knowledge that is merely useful background. A new hire needs the approval limit, the escalation contact, and the client preference, but does not need the history of every past project. Ask the manager to mark each item as must know, should know, or reference only. That ranking makes the remaining handover time realistic and keeps the receiver from drowning in context.

## Inventory the knowledge holders

List the people who currently supply onboarding knowledge, not only the departing manager. A team lead, a senior worker, a client contact, and a system administrator may each hold part of the picture. Mark the knowledge that has only one holder as the highest risk. Ask each holder to name the questions they answered for the last two new hires. Compare the lists and look for gaps. This step often reveals that the manager is not the only bottleneck, which changes the transfer plan. The goal is a map of where the knowledge lives, not a tribute to one person.

Include the client contacts who answer onboarding questions, because a receiver may need permission to contact them. Note the channel and the response expectation for each holder. A name without a working contact path is only a label. Update the map after the first handover session as people discover gaps that were invisible on paper.

## Measure coverage before departure

For each knowledge item, record whether it is documented, shared with a named receiver, or held only by the departing manager. Add a confidence field for whether the receiver has actually used the item. A document that exists but has never been tested may not be usable under pressure. Calculate the share of items that are both documented and receiver-tested. Report the three states separately rather than collapsing them into a single coverage percentage. The untested but documented group is where a quick exercise can produce the most improvement.

Give the receiver a short scenario that uses the document and ask them to explain the decision out loud. If they pause or search for a missing link, fix the document before the departure. Record the date and the person who tested it. A tested document is more valuable than a longer document that nobody has used.

## Test transfer with live decisions

Choose a set of recent onboarding decisions and ask the receiver to make them using only the transferred material. Include a normal access decision, a client preference, and an exception. Compare the receiver's answer with the manager's and record the reason for any gap. If the receiver needed to ask the manager, the knowledge was not transferred even if a document exists. Repeat the exercise with a new hire who is currently in onboarding, because that person will expose missing context faster than a peer. The test should happen before the manager's last week.

If the receiver cannot complete the test, reduce the scope or extend the handover rather than accepting a verbal assurance. Write the result in the transition log with the item, the gap, and the repair. Repeat the test after the repair with a different item. The second result shows whether the receiver can transfer the learning, not only whether one answer was corrected.

## Distinguish documented from accessible

A rule can be written and still be inaccessible. Check whether the receiver can find the document, open the linked system, and understand the terms. Search for the article using the words a new hire would use, not the words the author would use. Check whether the document is in the right language and reading level. Check whether access to the source system is approved. A knowledge item counts as accessible only when the receiver can reach it and use it during normal work. Accessibility is part of coverage, not a separate technical concern.

## Estimate the residual gap

After the transfer, list the items that remain undocumented or untested and estimate the consequence of each gap. A missing client preference may cause a small revision, while a missing approval limit may cause an unauthorized commitment. Rank the gaps by consequence and frequency. Name an owner for each gap and a date to close it. If a gap cannot be closed before the departure, arrange a named temporary advisor and a clear limit on how long that support will last. The residual gap is the honest measure of transition risk.

Review the list with the receiving manager and the client owner, because some gaps can be accepted while others need a temporary advisor. Set an expiry date for any temporary support so it does not become permanent. Report the gap count again after thirty days. A transition is complete when the receiver can handle the common decisions and knows where to go for the rest.

Write a short closeout note with the date, the receiver, the gaps that remain, and the date of the next check. Share it with the manager and the client owner so the support arrangement is visible. If a temporary advisor is still needed, state the limit and the exit condition. A closeout note that only says the handover is complete hides the risk the inventory was meant to expose.

## Report and prioritize

Report the inventory size, the documented share, the receiver-tested share, and the highest-consequence gaps. Add the number of new hires currently in onboarding and the dates they need the missing knowledge. Recommend a small set of actions such as recording one walkthrough, transferring a system owner role, or adding an approval to the written matrix. Assign an owner and a review date. Present the result to the manager's manager, not only to the team, because some gaps require a decision about staffing or client communication.

## Limitations

The inventory depends on what people remember and are willing to disclose, and some knowledge is tacit. A receiver may pass a test and still struggle with a real client or a rare exception. The method measures coverage of defined onboarding knowledge, not the overall competence of the departing manager. Small teams have limited redundancy, so a single gap can look larger than it would in a bigger group. State the scope, the sample of decisions, and the date of the test. Use the result to prioritize, not to declare that the transition is complete.
